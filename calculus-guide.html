Calculus Guide: Differentiation Made Simple

March 25, 2026 ⏱ 9 min read Apex STEM Tutors

Calculus is the mathematics of change — how things move, grow, and transform. Differentiation, the first pillar of calculus, answers the question: "How fast is this changing?" While the theory can seem abstract, the applications are everywhere: from predicting population growth to designing roller coasters. This guide breaks down differentiation into digestible concepts that build your intuition before diving into the formulas.

Lesson outline

Study notes at a glance

What you'll learn

  • How to interpret a derivative as a rate of change
  • How the power rule simplifies polynomial differentiation
  • When to use the chain rule and trigonometric rules

Key takeaway

Differentiation is about change, and every rule is just a shortcut for measuring that change clearly.

Study tip

The Big Idea: Rates of Change

What differentiation actually measures

Differentiation is fundamentally about measuring how quickly something changes. If you have a function f(x) that describes a relationship (like distance traveled over time), the derivative f'(x) tells you the instantaneous rate of change at any point.

Think of driving a car: your speedometer shows your instantaneous speed (the derivative of distance with respect to time). Average speed is distance/time, but instantaneous speed requires calculus.

The Derivative
f'(x) = limh→0 [f(x+h) - f(x)] / h
The slope of the tangent line at point x — how steep the curve is at that exact point.

Physics: Velocity

If s(t) = distance at time t, then v(t) = s'(t) = instantaneous velocity.

Economics: Marginal Cost

If C(x) = total cost for x units, C'(x) = cost of producing one more unit.

Biology: Population Growth

If P(t) = population at time t, P'(t) = growth rate at that moment.

Power Rule: The Workhorse

Differentiating polynomials

The power rule is the most frequently used differentiation rule. For any power function f(x) = xⁿ, the derivative is f'(x) = n x^(n-1). This rule handles polynomials, which form the basis of most calculus problems.

The intuition: when you have x³, differentiating "uses up" one x, leaving 3x². The 3 comes from the exponent, and you reduce the power by 1.

Power Rule
d/dx [xⁿ] = n x^(n-1)
Bring down the exponent, reduce power by 1. Works for any real number n.

d/dx [x⁵] = 5x⁴

Bring down 5, reduce exponent to 4.

d/dx [√x] = d/dx [x^(1/2)] = (1/2)x^(-1/2)

Works for fractional powers too.

d/dx [x] = d/dx [x¹] = 1×x⁰ = 1

Any constant to the power 1 differentiates to 1.

Sum, Product, and Chain Rules

Combining functions

Most functions aren't simple powers — they're combinations. The sum rule lets you differentiate term by term. The product rule handles multiplication. The chain rule is for composite functions (functions within functions).

These rules let you break down complex functions into simpler parts you already know how to differentiate.

Chain Rule
d/dx [f(g(x))] = f'(g(x)) × g'(x)
Derivative of outer function times derivative of inner function.

d/dx [sin(x²)] = cos(x²) × 2x

Outer: sin(u), derivative cos(u). Inner: x², derivative 2x.

d/dx [(x+1)³] = 3(x+1)² × 1

Outer: u³, derivative 3u². Inner: x+1, derivative 1.

d/dx [e^(x²)] = e^(x²) × 2x

Outer: e^u, derivative e^u. Inner: x², derivative 2x.

Trigonometric Derivatives

Circles and waves

Trigonometric functions describe circular motion and waves. Their derivatives are cyclical: sine differentiates to cosine, cosine to negative sine, and so on. These patterns repeat every 2π radians.

The key insight: trig derivatives are about phase shifts. Each differentiation shifts the function by 90° (π/2 radians) in the unit circle.

Trig Derivatives
d/dx [sin x] = cos x
d/dx [cos x] = -sin x, d/dx [tan x] = sec² x

d/dx [sin(3x)] = 3 cos(3x)

Chain rule: derivative of sin(u) is cos(u) × du/dx.

d/dx [cos² x] = 2 cos x (-sin x) = -2 cos x sin x

Chain rule plus product rule for cos² x.

d/dx [sin x / cos x] = d/dx [tan x] = sec² x

Quotient rule or recognize as tan x.

Exponential and Logarithmic

Growth and decay

Exponential functions model growth and decay. Their derivatives are special: e^x differentiates to itself. Logarithms are the inverse, and their derivatives involve 1/x.

This is why e is the "natural" base — it's the only number where the derivative equals the function itself.

Exponential Rule
d/dx [e^x] = e^x
d/dx [a^x] = a^x ln a, d/dx [ln x] = 1/x

d/dx [e^(2x)] = 2 e^(2x)

Chain rule: e^u differentiates to e^u × du/dx.

d/dx [ln(x²)] = (1/x²) × 2x = 2/x

Chain rule: ln(u) differentiates to (1/u) × du/dx.

d/dx [2^x] = 2^x ln 2

General exponential rule for base other than e.

Apex Insight

Calculus isn't about memorizing formulas — it's about recognizing patterns. Every differentiation problem is really just: "What rule applies here? How do the rules combine?" Master the five core rules above, and you can differentiate 90% of A-Level calculus problems. The rest is practice and pattern recognition.

Common Pitfalls & How to Avoid Them

These mistakes cost marks. Learn them now to avoid them later.

  • ️

    Forgetting the chain rule

    When you see a function within a function, you need the chain rule. d/dx [sin(x²)] ≠ cos(x²) — it needs the extra 2x.

  • ️

    Mixing up trig derivatives

    d/dx [sin x] = cos x, but d/dx [cos x] = -sin x. The negative sign is easy to forget.

  • ️

    Treating constants as variables

    Constants differentiate to zero. In d/dx [3x² + 7], the 7 disappears, and the 3 is a constant multiplier.

  • ️

    Product rule confusion

    d/dx [f g] = f' g + f g'. Remember: first times derivative of second, plus second times derivative of first.

  • ️

    Quotient rule complexity

    d/dx [f/g] = (f' g - f g') / g². It's easier to rewrite as a product: f/g = f × g^(-1), then use product and chain rules.

Medicine

The Path Forward

Differentiation is a skill built through deliberate practice. Start with the power rule, master the chain rule, then add the others. Don't just memorize — understand why each rule works. Work through examples until the patterns become automatic.
